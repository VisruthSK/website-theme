A river is a record of everything upstream. The colour of the water, the size of the stones on its bed and the shape of its banks all carry information about rain that fell weeks ago and rock that broke apart centuries before that. Learning to read a river means learning to treat it as a slow instrument, one that has been taking measurements for longer than anyone has been around to check them.

The first thing most people notice is speed. Water moves faster in the middle of a channel than near the banks, and faster near the surface than near the bed, because friction drags at the edges. Hydrologists have measured this for over a century, and the classic profile is surprisingly regular [@placeholder2019]. A floating leaf tells you about the surface; a pebble rolling along the bottom tells you about the bed; the difference between them tells you how rough the channel is.

## Discharge

The quantity that matters most is discharge, the volume of water passing a point each second. It is the product of the channel's cross-sectional area and the mean velocity of the water, written simply as $Q = A \bar{v}$. Measuring either factor is harder than the formula suggests. Area changes with every flood, and velocity changes from one side of the channel to the other, so a single reading is never enough.

Field teams solve this by dividing the channel into strips, measuring the depth and velocity in each, and adding up the results:

$$
Q \approx \sum_{i=1}^{n} d_i \, w_i \, v_i
$$

Each term is a narrow slice of the river, and the sum approaches the true discharge as the slices get thinner. The method is old, tedious and very reliable, which is why it is still used to calibrate the automatic gauges that now line most large rivers [@sample2021, p. 14].

:::note[Units]
Discharge is usually reported in cubic metres per second. A small stream might carry less than one; a large river in flood can carry tens of thousands.
:::

## Sediment

Moving water carries material, and the amount it can carry rises steeply with speed. A gentle current moves only fine silt; a fast one rolls cobbles along the bed. When the current slows, at a bend or where the river meets the sea, the material settles out in order of size, coarse gravel first and fine mud last. The result is a sorted deposit that records the speed of the water that laid it down.

Geologists use these deposits to reconstruct floods that no one saw. A layer of large boulders far from the present channel says that the river once ran much faster or much higher than it does now; a fine layer of clay says that the water once stood still long enough for the smallest particles to fall. Read in sequence, the layers become a history of wet and dry years stretching back thousands of years [see @placeholder2019; @example2020].

## Meanders

Left alone on flat ground, rivers rarely run straight. A small disturbance pushes the current toward one bank, the water erodes that bank and deposits material on the opposite side, and the bend grows. Over time the bends migrate downstream and outward, until two of them meet and the river cuts across the narrow neck between them, leaving the old loop behind as a crescent-shaped lake.

What is striking about meanders is how similar they look regardless of size. A creek a few metres wide and a river a kilometre across produce bends with roughly the same proportions, as if the shape depended only on the width of the channel. This regularity has been studied with flumes, with computer models and with photographs taken from aircraft, and the explanation is still debated [-@example2020]. Rivers, it seems, have a sense of proportion that we are only beginning to understand.

## Listening

None of this requires instruments to appreciate. Standing on a bank and watching the water for a while is enough to see most of it: the fast thread in the middle, the eddies behind rocks, the fine sand collecting where the current slows. The river is always explaining itself, to anyone patient enough to watch, and the explanation changes with every season.

Some of the best observers of rivers have been people who simply lived beside them for decades: millers, ferry operators, fishers. Their knowledge was rarely written down, but it was precise, and much of what hydrology later formalised was already familiar to them. A river rewards attention, and attention, unlike instruments, is available to everyone.
