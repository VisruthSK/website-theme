Mixing dough is easy; keeping its structure intact through shaping, proving and baking is where most loaves go wrong. Overnight fermentation fills the dough with small bubbles of gas held in a stretchy web of gluten, and every step after that is about preserving the web while giving the loaf a shape that will hold up in the oven.

Shaping is the step that took me longest to learn. The goal is to tighten the outside of the dough into a smooth skin without pressing the gas out of the inside, which means working quickly and lightly, with a bench scraper doing most of the work. The first dozen attempts produced loaves that spread sideways in the oven like puddles. The next dozen produced loaves that held their shape but were dense, because I had pressed too hard. Somewhere around the thirtieth week the motion became automatic.

Proving is the final rise before baking, and it is the easiest step to get wrong in either direction. Underproved dough bursts open unpredictably in the oven; overproved dough collapses because the gluten has stretched as far as it can. The test I settled on was simple: press a floured finger into the dough and watch how quickly the dent fills back in. A slow, partial recovery means the dough is ready.

Baking itself is mostly about heat and steam. A very hot oven sets the crust quickly, and steam in the first few minutes keeps the surface soft long enough for the loaf to expand. A covered pot does both jobs at once, trapping the moisture from the dough and radiating heat from every side. The lid comes off after twenty minutes, and the crust darkens over the next twenty.

The hardest step comes last: waiting for the loaf to cool before cutting it. Bread is still cooking for an hour after it leaves the oven, as steam escapes and the crumb sets. Cut it early and the inside is gummy; wait, and it is everything the previous day of work was for.

Scoring the loaf just before it goes into the oven is the last decision, and the most visible one. A shallow cut at a low angle opens into a raised ear of crust; a deep vertical cut opens wide and flat. The pattern matters less than the confidence of the blade: a hesitant cut drags at the dough and tears it, while a quick one slices cleanly and lets the loaf expand where you want it to.

Over the year the loaves became more consistent, but never identical. Every bake was slightly different, depending on the flour, the weather and how closely I had been paying attention. That variation stopped bothering me after a while. Bread made by hand is supposed to differ from week to week; the uniformity of a factory loaf is the thing that would be strange.


A note for anyone attempting the same: write down what you did while you are doing it, not afterwards. Memory is generous to the parts that went well and quietly forgets the rest, and the forgotten parts are usually the ones worth learning from.
