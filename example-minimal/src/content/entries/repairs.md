---
title: 'Small Repairs'
date: 2025-03-22
description: 'Fixing a chair, and what it taught me about patience.'
tags: ['making']
---

The kitchen chair had wobbled for a year before I did anything about it. One leg had worked loose from its socket, and every time someone sat down the joint opened a little further. A proper repair meant taking the chair apart, cleaning out the old glue and putting it back together, which sounded like an afternoon's work and turned into three evenings.

The first evening was spent on disassembly. Old glue does not want to let go, and the joints had to be eased apart with gentle taps of a mallet and a lot of patience. The temptation to hit harder was constant; giving in to it would have split the wood. By the end of the evening the chair was a pile of parts on the table, each one labelled with tape so that it would go back in the same place.

The second evening was cleaning: scraping the old glue out of every socket and off every tenon until bare wood showed through. It is dull work and impossible to hurry, because new glue will not hold on old glue, and a joint that is not clean will fail again within a year. I listened to the radio and scraped.

The third evening was assembly. Glue on every joint, parts fitted together in the right order, clamps tightened until a thin bead of glue squeezed out along each seam, and then nothing to do but wait. The chair sat clamped on the kitchen floor overnight, looking more like an instrument of torture than a piece of furniture.

In the morning it was solid. No wobble, no creak, no sign that it had ever been broken. It is a small thing, but there is a particular satisfaction in fixing something properly rather than replacing it, and in knowing that the chair will probably outlast me.

Repairs like this used to be ordinary. A household kept glue, clamps and a few tools, and things were fixed when they broke because replacing them was expensive. Now replacement is usually cheaper than an evening of work, at least on paper, and the skills that made repair routine have faded. The chair cost very little when it was new; the three evenings were worth more than the chair.

And yet the evenings were not really a cost. They were quiet, absorbing and slightly meditative, the kind of work where the hands are busy and the mind is free to wander. By the end I knew the chair better than any other piece of furniture in the house: which joints were original, which had been glued before by someone else, where the wood had been patched long ago with a careful sliver of a different timber.

The next thing on the list is a drawer that sticks in damp weather. It will probably take longer than it should, and I am looking forward to it.


A note for anyone attempting the same: write down what you did while you are doing it, not afterwards. Memory is generous to the parts that went well and quietly forgets the rest, and the forgotten parts are usually the ones worth learning from.
