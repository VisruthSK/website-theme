---
title: 'Winter Light'
date: 2025-01-12
description: 'Notes on the short days and how to spend them.'
tags: ['seasons']
---

In the middle of winter the sun barely clears the roofs across the street. It rises late, crawls along the horizon for a few hours and sets in the middle of the afternoon, and the light it gives is low, gold and strangely bright. Everything it touches throws a long shadow; everything it misses is grey.

I have started arranging my days around that light. The hours between ten and two are for being outside, whatever the weather, because they are the only hours when the world looks properly lit. Work that needs a lamp anyway can wait for the dark. It is an old habit, older than electric lighting, and it makes the short days feel less like a loss and more like a schedule.

Winter light is also the best light for reading the land. With the leaves gone and the sun low, every bump and hollow in a field casts a shadow, and old boundaries, ridges and paths that are invisible in summer stand out clearly. Walking the same route in December and in June is like reading two different maps of the same place.

The worst of the season is not the cold or even the dark but the grey days in between, when cloud sits on the hills and the light never quite arrives. On those days the best advice I have found is to go out anyway, briefly, and come back to a warm room and a pot of tea.[^tea] The contrast does most of the work.

By the end of January the days are noticeably longer, and by February the sun is clearing the roofs again. The turn of the year happens long before the weather admits it, and noticing it early is one of the small pleasures of paying attention.

[^tea]: Any tea will do, but strong black tea seems to suit grey weather best.

The short days change what counts as an achievement. In summer an afternoon walk is nothing special; in December it is a small expedition that has to be planned around the weather and the clock. Getting out before the light fails feels like winning something, and the walk home in the blue dusk, with lamps coming on in kitchen windows, is one of the better hours of the year.

Indoors, the season is good for slow work. Letters that have waited since autumn get answered, drawers get sorted, and the books that were too long for summer finally get read. There is no pressure to be outside, because outside is dark by four, and the evenings stretch out long enough to finish things that have been half done for months.

Some years I have tried to fight the season with bright lamps and early alarms. It never works for long. The body knows it is winter whatever the clock says, and the easier path is to go along with it: sleep a little more, eat a little more, and save the ambitious plans for the spring.


A note for anyone attempting the same: write down what you did while you are doing it, not afterwards. Memory is generous to the parts that went well and quietly forgets the rest, and the forgotten parts are usually the ones worth learning from.
