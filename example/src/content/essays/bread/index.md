At the start of last year I decided to bake one loaf of bread every week and write down what happened. The rule was simple: same flour, same oven, one change at a time. The point was not to become a baker but to understand why the loaves I had made in the past were so unpredictable, sometimes excellent and sometimes flat, with no obvious reason for the difference.

The first month was humbling. Bread turns out to be a negotiation between yeast, flour, water and time, and every one of those parties has opinions. Yeast is fussy about temperature, flour varies from bag to bag, water quantities that work in winter are too generous in summer, and time is the variable everyone underestimates. A loaf that has been rushed looks fine going into the oven and disappointing coming out.

By the third month a pattern had emerged. The good loaves were the slow ones: dough mixed in the evening, left overnight somewhere cool, shaped in the morning and baked in the afternoon. The flat loaves were the ones squeezed into a single afternoon because I had forgotten to plan ahead. Nothing about the recipe mattered as much as the clock.

The middle of the year was spent on details: how wet the dough could be before it became impossible to shape, how long it needed to rest between folds, how hot the oven had to be for a crust that crackled rather than one that merely browned. Each experiment changed one thing and kept the rest the same, and the notebook filled with small tables of times and temperatures that are much less interesting to read than they were to collect.

What I learned, in the end, was less about bread than about patience. Most of the work happens while nothing seems to be happening; the dough is changing in ways you cannot see, and interfering only makes it worse. The chapters that follow describe the two parts of the process that taught me the most: keeping a starter alive, and getting a loaf from the bowl to the table without losing the air that took all night to build.

There is no secret recipe at the end, only a habit. One loaf a week, written down, adds up to a surprising amount of understanding by December, and an even more surprising amount of bread.

The notebook from that year is now stained with flour and water and is barely legible in places. It is still the most useful cookbook I own, because every page records something that actually happened in my kitchen, with my flour and my oven, rather than something that worked once for somebody else. Recipes are a starting point; a record of your own attempts is a map of the territory.

If you want to try the same experiment, the only equipment you need is a scale, a bowl, a pot with a lid and a notebook. The scale matters more than anything else, because bread is a matter of proportions, and cups and spoons are too imprecise to tell you why one week worked and the next did not.


A note for anyone attempting the same: write down what you did while you are doing it, not afterwards. Memory is generous to the parts that went well and quietly forgets the rest, and the forgotten parts are usually the ones worth learning from.
