This essay began as a set of notes for a talk. It has been expanded and lightly edited, but the order of the sections follows the talk.

The talk itself was given to a small audience on a wet evening, which seemed appropriate.

---

For most of history, the coast was the most dangerous place a ship could be. Open water is wide and forgiving; it is the last few miles before a harbour, with their rocks, shoals and shifting sandbanks, that wrecked vessels by the thousand. The lighthouse was the answer to that danger, and for two centuries it was one of the most important pieces of engineering a nation could build.

The earliest lights were simply fires. A beacon on a headland, fed with wood or coal, told sailors where the land was and, if they knew the coast, roughly where they were. Fires were cheap and easy to build, but they were also dim, smoky and impossible to tell apart. A ship approaching at night could see a glow on the shore and still have no idea which glow it was, or which rocks lay beneath it.

## Lenses and mirrors

The great change came with optics. Polished reflectors placed behind oil lamps gathered light that would otherwise have spilled into the sky and sent it out toward the sea, multiplying the brightness many times over. Later, stepped glass lenses made it possible to concentrate light even further, into beams that could be seen more than twenty miles away on a clear night. The lenses were heavy, expensive and beautiful, assembled from dozens of carefully ground prisms set in brass frames.

Brightness solved one problem and made another obvious. If every light shone the same way, sailors still could not tell which one they were looking at. The solution was to give each light a signature: a pattern of flashes, eclipses and colours that could be timed with a watch and looked up in a printed list. "Two white flashes every ten seconds" meant one headland; "a steady red light" meant another. A navigator who could count could place himself on the chart.

## The keepers

Lamps needed people. Wicks had to be trimmed, oil carried up winding stairs, lenses polished every morning and clockwork wound every few hours through the night. A light that failed for even an hour could cost lives, so keepers worked in shifts and kept careful logs of every hour of darkness. The job was lonely, repetitive and, on the more exposed stations, genuinely dangerous.

Some stations stood on rocks so isolated that keepers were relieved only every few weeks, weather permitting. Storms could delay relief for a month or more, and the logbooks from those weeks make grim reading: rations stretched, tempers frayed, the same three people looking at the same grey sea. Other stations were on the mainland, with gardens, families and visitors in the summer. The romance of the lighthouse keeper comes mostly from the first kind; the reality was usually closer to the second.

## Automation

Electricity changed the work, and then ended it. Electric lamps were brighter and needed less attention than oil; automatic switches could turn them on at dusk and off at dawn; remote monitoring could report a fault before anyone on a ship noticed it. One by one, through the second half of the twentieth century, the stations were automated and the keepers withdrawn.

Today most lighthouses run without anyone living in them. Satellite navigation has made them less essential than they once were, though they remain an important backup when electronics fail. Many of the old keepers' cottages have become museums or holiday lets, and some towers are open to visitors who want to climb the stairs that keepers once climbed several times a night.

## What remains

It is easy to be sentimental about lighthouses, and perhaps that is no bad thing. They are among the few pieces of public infrastructure built purely to protect strangers: no one paid to look at them, and the people they saved never knew the names of those who kept them lit. A lighthouse makes no profit and asks for no thanks. It simply stands at the edge of the land and shines, so that people it will never meet can find their way home.

That is a good thing for a building to do, and a good thing for a life to be spent on. The keepers are gone, but their logbooks survive, and reading them is a reminder that reliability is a kind of care: unglamorous, repeated every night, and noticed only when it fails.
