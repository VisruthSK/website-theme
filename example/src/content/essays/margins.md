Every printed page is an argument about emptiness. The words sit in the middle, but the decisions that make them readable happen at the edges, in the band of paper that nobody reads and everybody notices. A margin that is too narrow makes a book feel crowded and cheap; one that is too wide makes it feel precious, as if the text were an exhibit rather than something meant to be held. Somewhere between those two failures lies a width that disappears, and finding it has occupied printers for as long as there have been pages to print.

The oldest advice is also the simplest. Leave more room at the bottom than at the top, and more on the outside than toward the spine. The reasons are practical before they are aesthetic: thumbs need somewhere to rest, and the inner margins of two facing pages add together across the fold, so each one can be smaller than its outer twin.[^thumbs] What began as a concession to hands and bindings became, over centuries, a sense of proportion that readers feel without being able to name. A page laid out the other way round looks subtly wrong, like a picture hung too high on a wall.

## Measure and rhythm

The margin decides the measure, and the measure decides the rhythm of reading. A line that runs too long forces the eye to travel so far that it loses its place on the return trip; a line that is too short breaks phrases apart and makes the reader jump every few words. Most typographers settle on something between forty-five and seventy-five characters, and they are right to be vague about it, because the best length depends on the typeface, the size and the leading. "Rules of thumb," as one compositor liked to say, "are for people who have stopped looking."

What the measure cannot do on its own is make the right edge of a justified column look even. When every line must end at the same place, the spaces between words have to stretch or shrink to make up the difference, and a careless setting produces rivers of white running down the page. Good composition looks at a whole paragraph at once and chooses breaks that keep the spacing steady from line to line. It is slow work for a person and quick work for a program, which is why it took so long for screens to catch up with what hand compositors did as a matter of course.

There is a second, quieter trick. Punctuation at the end of a line carries less ink than a letter, so a column whose full stops and commas sit exactly on the edge looks ragged even when it is mathematically straight. Letting those marks hang slightly into the margin restores the optical edge.[^hanging] The same is true at the start of a line, where an opening quotation mark or a round capital such as **O** can be nudged outward until the column appears to begin in a single vertical stroke.

## The cost of crowding

When paper was expensive, margins shrank. Cheap editions of novels, printed in long runs on thin stock, squeezed every possible line onto every page, and readers paid for the savings with their eyes. The habit survived into the early web, where pages stretched from one side of the window to the other and text ran edge to edge on any screen wide enough to allow it. It took years for designers to rediscover that a narrow column in the middle of a wide window is not wasted space but the whole point.

Crowding also affects how we think about a text. A page with generous margins invites annotation; it suggests that the reader has a part to play. Medieval manuscripts were covered in glosses, corrections and doodles, many of them more interesting than the text they surrounded. A modern paperback offers no such invitation, and a phone screen offers even less. The margin, in other words, is where a page admits that it is part of a conversation.

## Notes in the margin

That conversation is the reason some writers prefer side notes to footnotes. A footnote sends the reader to the bottom of the page and back again, a small journey that breaks concentration every time it is made. A note set beside the text, level with the line it explains, can be read with a sideways glance and ignored just as easily.[^glance] The cost is width: side notes need a margin wide enough to hold them, which is exactly the kind of margin that cheap printing removed.

On a screen the trade looks different. A wide window has room to spare on either side of a comfortable column, and putting notes there costs nothing. A narrow window has no room at all, so the notes have to go somewhere else, usually to the end of the piece, where they behave like ordinary footnotes. The same text, in other words, can be laid out two ways depending on where it is read, and a well-made page switches between them without the reader noticing the seam.

## Proportion without numbers

It is tempting to look for a formula. Renaissance printers drew diagonals across their pages and placed the text block where the lines crossed; later designers used ratios borrowed from music, or from the proportions of the page itself. These systems produce handsome results, but they are descriptions of good taste rather than its source. A designer who has looked at enough pages will reach a similar answer by eye, and will know when to break the rule because the typeface is darker than usual or the paper is unusually small.

The `measure` property of a modern layout is a number, but the judgement behind it is not. One can set a column to sixty-six characters and still end up with a page that feels cramped, because the leading is too tight or the margins are too thin to frame it. The only reliable test is to read: print the page, or open it on the device where it will be read, and spend a few minutes with it. Discomfort shows up quickly, often before one can say what is wrong.

## What the edge is for

A margin, finally, is a promise of attention. It tells the reader that someone thought about the shape of the page before filling it, that the words were placed rather than poured. Readers rarely notice a good margin, but they feel its absence immediately, in the way a page seems to push at them instead of waiting to be read. The best compliment a layout can receive is silence: no one remarks on it, and everyone finishes the page.
